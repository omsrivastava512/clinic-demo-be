## 1. Package Management Features

**Hold.** Admin/owner-only action. The record should not carry an explicit "on hold" flag or duration — attendance shows as separate date-range segments instead (e.g. "first 6 days: Jul 1–6," "remaining 4 days: Aug 9–12"), and the gap between segments is how a hold reads in the history, implicitly. Current `packages` columns (`day_log jsonb`, `duration_days`, `expiry_date`, `attended_days`/`missed_days`) don't represent multiple discontinuous active segments today — this needs its own design pass, not solved here.

**Cancel + Refund.** Admin/owner-only, same restriction as Hold. Formula, resolved (Section 3.1): refund = package total − (days used × the regular, non-discounted rate). Covers both an early "emergency, can't continue" cancellation and a package that's mostly used with some missed days along the way. Suggested button label: "Cancel Package" — matches the owner's own term from the original conversation, and reads more accurately than "Refund" alone, since the action is really terminating the package, of which a refund is a consequence rather than the whole action.

*Edge case — reassigning remaining days to a different complaint.* Rare (patient wants their last few package days applied to a different body part than the one the package was sold for). Recommended handling: cancel the original package honestly, with ₹0 refunded — money didn't leave the business, it's being reallocated, which is different from "nothing happened" and different from an actual cash refund. Keeps the record accurate rather than quietly logging the wrong body part as completed, which would work against the whole point of the flag/red-zone feature down the line. For the reallocated days themselves: open a real new complaint course and log real visits, with those specific visits' charges waived (see Section 7), rather than handling it fully off the record — a flag next to an actual visible pattern in the visit history is far more useful to future staff than a flag backed only by a text note. Given how rare this is, no dedicated UI flow for it — handle it as ordinary manual admin steps when it comes up, not a guided feature.

**Flag / Red Zone.** Manual only — Om was explicit the app should never auto-flag; a staff/admin member flags a patient and types a reason (disputes, abusive behavior, "has a lawyer, be careful" were the examples given). Needs to be visually unmissable on the patient's profile and needs flagged patients surfaced near the top of some list, so staff don't miss it years later. Stated purpose is closer to institutional memory/character-reference than clinical alerting.

**Resolved:** a dedicated `patient_flags` table, not an extension of `patient_alerts`. Different audience and different sensitivity — `patient_alerts` is clinical safety information a clinician needs mid-treatment (allergy, fall risk, DNR); a red-zone flag is an administrative/behavioral caution for whoever handles scheduling and money, not medical information, and mixing the two risks burying real medical alerts among behavioral notes, or exposing a sensitive judgment call about someone's conduct to everyone with any reason to check clinical alerts. `patient_alerts` also has no `created_by` column at all today, while a flag specifically needs accountability for who made the call.

Shape: `patient_id`, `owner_id` (same `derive_owner_id_from_patient()` pattern as alerts/vitals/notes/timeline — a difficult patient is difficult chain-wide, not branch-specific), `flagged_by` (who), `reason` (full text, not a short label), `created_at`. Display needs nothing extra in the schema — a patient with any row here gets a marker wherever their profile renders, and the "red zone" list is just a query for patients with at least one row, no separate boolean or cache needed since this isn't read on a hot billing path.

**Still open, not assumed either way:** should flagging be admin-only (same restriction as Hold/Cancel), or can any staff member flag a patient? The original conversation never said. Front-desk staff are the ones most likely to hit the friction this feature exists for, which argues for letting them flag directly — but it's also a weighty, permanent, visible judgment about a specific person, which argues for admin-only. Om's call.

## 2. Fee Restructuring

**Visit types.** Already in the schema as-is — `visits.visit_type CHECK (visit_type in ('CONSULTATION', 'MACHINE_ONLY'))`. Confirmed by Om's description as the real division, not a new concept to add. The two types bill completely differently. Important structural point, confirmed: this lives on `visits`, not on `clinics` — decided per visit, not locked in per clinic or per patient. The same patient can get a MACHINE_ONLY visit one day and a CONSULTATION visit the next with zero schema conflict, since nothing above the individual visit row ever fixes which model applies. This already covers the "what if billing model needs to vary by patient/visit rather than by clinic" scenario Om raised — no schema change needed for it. Follow-up decision: add `clinics.default_visit_type` purely as a frontend pre-fill convenience for the visit-creation form (cheap, no enforcement needed, just a starting value) — build this. A "lock" concept (preventing receptionists from ever changing it per-visit, only admins editing a clinic-level default) was considered and set aside for now — it needs real enforcement across schema, RLS, and frontend for a complaint no clinic has actually raised yet. Add it later, as a single ordinary RLS policy, if and when a specific clinic asks for it.

**MACHINE_ONLY visits.** Itemized, per-machine billing. Every machine used (IFT, TENS, ultrasound, infrared, wax therapy, others) has its own price in a catalog; a visit's charge is the sum of whichever machines were actually used, like a cart. Maps directly onto the existing `services` + `visit_services` + `clinic_service_prices` architecture — confirmed by Om directly, not just an inference.

**Doctor-directed visits — still needs a settled name.** Om flagged "consultation therapy" as a placeholder, not final. This is the non-itemized visit type: the clinician has full discretion over which machines a complaint gets, all covered by one flat fee rather than priced per machine. A small set of specialty services sit outside this flat-fee bundling entirely, regardless of visit type — dry needling, cupping therapy, and laser therapy were named as examples, each with its own separate price chart. A screenshot is coming to clarify the full list.

*Old model (doctor-directed visits):* first-time consultation = ₹300 flat, bundling that day's exam and therapy into one number, clinician's choice of machines included. Subsequent/follow-up visits = ₹200 flat, same bundling logic. The specialty carve-outs sat outside this in both cases.

*New model (doctor-directed visits):* first-time consultation = ₹350, now exam-only, no therapy included. If the patient also takes therapy that same day — the default; most patients do unless they explicitly decline — therapy bills separately, **per body part / per complaint**, at ₹200 each, clinician's choice of machines still included within that flat per-complaint rate (not itemized by machine). Two complaints treated same day = 350 (once, shared) + 200 + 200 = ₹750. Subsequent/follow-up visits stay ₹200 per complaint being treated that day — unchanged from the old model; only the first-time bundling changed. Specialty carve-outs are unchanged in both models.

**Gap-return policy (10 consecutive missed days).** Specific to this clinic — Om flagged it himself as unusual and isn't sure how to make it scalable to other clinics later. If a patient misses sessions for 10 *consecutive* days mid-course (a lapse inside an ongoing complaint, not "10 days since ever first visiting"), the next visit on return is billed differently from both a true first-timer and an ordinary follow-up. **Corrected:** every complaint being treated still gets its normal per-complaint therapy rate (200/300 by tier), no exceptions, and a separate, flat, one-time ₹150 lapse penalty is added on top, once per visit regardless of how many complaints are involved. One complaint, gap-return: 200+150=350. Two complaints, gap-return: 200+200+150=550 (Om's own confirmed number). This is a different shape from the true first-time fee (350 exam-only, then +200 per complaint if therapy is taken) — an earlier version of this entry incorrectly modeled the gap-return fee as a bundled 350 in the same shape as the exam fee; corrected here. Three states total, confirmed: first-time (350 exam once + 200/300 per complaint if therapy taken), normal follow-up (200/300 per complaint, no flat add-on), gap-return (200/300 per complaint + 150 flat, once).

*Multi-complaint gap-return* — resolved, using Om's own numbers above: the 150 lapse penalty is shared once across however many complaints reactivate together, same logic as the exam fee being shared across multiple same-day complaints on a true first visit. Recommended build: three additive rules, not branches — (1) always add the per-complaint rate for every complaint treated; (2) if first-touchpoint-ever, add 350 once; (3) if first-touchpoint-after-a-gap instead, add 150 once. Rules 2 and 3 are mutually exclusive on any given visit, but neither depends on how many complaints are involved, so the same three rules cover every complaint-count automatically.

*Waiver mechanism* — full design moved to Section 7 (computed-vs-actual pricing columns, the three override scopes, and the authorization-mechanism comparison).

**Rehab tier — confirmed, not inferred.** Rehab-category complaints (fracture, post-surgery, post-op, neuro/paralysis) have a genuinely separate rate table covering both daily visit pricing and package pricing — "everything changes" for a rehab complaint, per Om directly. Whether the ₹350 exam fee itself varies by tier: for now, no — flat 350 regardless of complaint category, confirmed by Om. Decided: build it tier-aware anyway, as a column on whatever table ends up holding the confirmed tier-varying rates (see the `clinic_tier_rates` idea in Section 6), with both tiers set to 350 for now. Costs nothing structurally since that table already needs to exist for the daily/package rates; avoids a migration later if the numbers ever do diverge.

**Old two-column mapping, confirmed.** `clinics.consultation_fee_first_in_paise` / `consultation_fee_subsequent_in_paise` (300/200) is confirmed as exactly the old bundled model. Under the new rule that becomes: a first-time exam-only fee (350) plus a separate per-complaint therapy fee (200 regular-tier default) that used to be folded into the 300 and no longer is. Om explicitly said not to fixate on the exact rupee figures from here on — they're likely to keep moving before this is final with the owner. The stable part to design around is the shape: decoupled exam fee, per-complaint therapy fee, tier-dependent therapy/package rates, a third gap-return state, and itemized machine-only billing as a fully separate visit path.

## 5. Schema / Architecture Notes — Brainstorm, Not a Decision

*Nothing here is settled. Treat it the way the invoice/session Option A/B/C decision gets treated — written up and decided deliberately, not inferred from a chat reply.*

**Machines are basically already solved.** Seed each machine/modality as a `services` row, let `clinic_service_prices` handle any per-clinic price differences, and "which machines were used in this visit" is exactly what `visit_services` already represents. No new junction table needed — just seed data and a clinic-level flag for whether that clinic bills this way.

**The therapy tier fee might belong in the same place, not a new `visits` column.** Rather than adding a dedicated `visits.therapy_fee_in_paise`, the base session itself could be a `services` row too — "Regular Physio Session" at 200, "Rehab Physio Session" at 300 — auto-attached to `visit_services` based on the complaint's tier, alongside whatever optional add-ons get logged normally. Reuses the catalog + override architecture that's already built, rather than inventing a third money bucket on `visits` next to `consultation_fee_in_paise` and `services_total_in_paise`. A new column would also work, just duplicates a pattern that already exists elsewhere.

**Complaint tiers need a representation:** either a CHECK-constrained enum on `complaint_courses` (cheap, matches the `services.category` precedent, but a new tier later means a migration) or a small lookup table FK'd from `complaint_courses` (closer to `clinic_service_prices`'s shape, no migration needed to add a third tier). Given tiers are explicitly "his business, he decides," and given the point of this whole conversation is avoiding rework later, the lookup-table version leans more future-proof — but this is exactly the kind of call worth making in writing, not deciding here.

**The shared ₹350 is the same open problem as the invoice/session gap already in the M1 log.** `invoices.visit_id` being a singular FK, and the schema having no session/encounter concept, was already flagged before this conversation — Option A (one invoice per visit, frontend batches), B (a junction table), C (session as frontend-only, never persisted, leaning direction at the time). The rule that consultation is shared across every complaint billed the same day is the *same* "what counts as one session" question, arriving from the billing side instead of the invoicing side — and it makes Option C harder to hold onto as-is, since something (a trigger, most likely) now needs to know "is this visit the first billing touchpoint of today's session" to decide whether ₹350 applies, which is exactly the kind of state Option C says shouldn't be persisted anywhere. Worth reopening that decision with this new information before committing to either the tier design or the visit-fee design above — they both hang off the same unresolved question.

**Packages likely need the tier link too,** per 3.3 — if that cross-reference is real, wherever package pricing ends up living needs the same tier reference `complaint_courses` gets.

**Not addressed here:** Hold's date-segment representation. Not enough detail yet on how `day_log` actually gets populated and read to responsibly propose a fix in the same pass as everything else — feels like its own focused design conversation once the rest of this settles.

## 6. Configurability Architecture — Coupled vs. Decoupled

*Still brainstorm-stage, same caveat as Section 5 — write this up and decide deliberately when it's time, don't treat anything here as settled.*

**The constraint, as Om set it.** No configuration UI for the MVP. Every rate and rule stays a fixed, seed-level value, editable only through direct database access. The open question isn't whether to build configurability now — it's how to shape the schema so a future config module touches as few tables as possible when it does get built.

**The actual test, not a blanket rule either way:** does this specific value vary along more than one dimension at once? A value that's genuinely 1:1 with its natural owner row belongs as a plain column on that row — no table needed, no over-engineering. A value that varies along two or more dimensions simultaneously (by clinic *and* by tier, say) can't be represented as a column without the column count growing every time a new tier or a new clinic-specific case shows up — exactly the maintenance burden this whole conversation is trying to avoid later.

**Applying that test to what's actually on the table:**
- Machine prices, and the specialty always-separate services (dry needling, cupping, laser): vary by clinic *and* by service — two dimensions. Already solved. `services` (base catalog) + `clinic_service_prices` (per-clinic override, `COALESCE`-falling-back to base) is exactly this pattern, already built, already proven elsewhere in the schema. Just seed more rows — no new structure.
- The ₹350 exam fee: confirmed tier-independent in value for now, but decided to live on the same tier-rate table as the values below rather than staying a plain `clinics` column — see the resolution in Section 2. Costs nothing extra, since that table exists regardless for the daily/package rates.
- Therapy day-rate and package rate: vary by tier for certain (200 vs 300, confirmed), and possibly by clinic too if the locality idea below ever gets used. Two dimensions in waiting — same shape as the services problem, different subject. This is the one place worth a small dedicated table — something like `clinic_tier_rates` (clinic_id, tier, daily_rate_in_paise, package_rate_in_paise) — rather than a growing set of columns on `clinics` (`regular_daily_rate`, `rehab_daily_rate`, `regular_package_rate`, `rehab_package_rate`, and a fresh pair every time a third tier shows up).

**On locality:** not actually a new problem — the same `clinic_id`-scoping pattern already used everywhere else in this schema (`clinic_service_prices`, the consultation-fee columns, the whole RLS model). Whatever ends up holding the tier-rate matrix just needs a `clinic_id` column and it's already locality-aware, same as `clinic_service_prices` already is. Nothing new to invent.

**On the env-file idea:** worth steering away from, specifically. An env file is read once at process start — a receptionist's screen and a database trigger computing a bill wouldn't see a change until a redeploy or restart, not a simple `UPDATE`. It also can't be joined against in SQL, so a trigger trying to look up "this complaint's tier rate at this clinic" would have no way to read it directly — the values would need to be mirrored into the database anyway to be usable, making the env file a redundant second source of truth rather than a simplification. Env files are the right tool for deployment secrets and values that are genuinely the same for every user of the whole app; per-clinic, queryable, dashboard-editable business rates are a database's job.

**On "map each number to its specific table":** right some of the time, not universally. Correct for the genuinely 1:1 values — services, and the flat clinic-level fee if it stays tier-independent — and it's already what the schema does today for those. It runs into trouble specifically where a value depends on more than one thing at once, which is exactly the tier situation. A small table scoped to that one multi-dimensional case isn't the same thing as "a separate table with all the numbers" in the way a single sprawling generic settings table would be — it's one narrowly-scoped table for one specific kind of variation, much cheaper to reason about later than either a pile of columns or one giant key-value blob.

## 7. Pricing Integrity & Override Architecture

*Brainstorm-stage, same caveat as Sections 5 and 6.*

**Correction, important:** an earlier version of this entry cited `derive_clinic_id_from_patient()` as an existing example of this pattern. That function does not exist in the current schema — it was a real function briefly in v3, got gutted to a no-op the moment `patients.clinic_id` was removed in v4 (patients aren't scoped to one clinic — they belong to a chain via `owner_id`, with per-branch attendance tracked separately in `patient_clinic_access`), and was replaced, not restored, in v5. Citing it as live was a mistake — conflated with the function that actually does exist and does this job: `derive_owner_id_from_patient()`, used on `patient_alerts`/`patient_vitals`/`clinical_notes`/`timeline_events`, which silently overwrites whatever `owner_id` a client submits with the value derived fresh from the parent patient row, every time. **The core principle.** Any amount that follows a fixed rule (consultation fee, per-complaint therapy rate, the lapse penalty, a refund) should be computed by the backend from its own rate tables, every time, regardless of what a client request contains — never trusted as a number the frontend sends. `derive_owner_id_from_patient()` is the real, live precedent for this pattern — the same discipline just needs extending to fee columns. Currently it is NOT extended that far: `visits.consultation_fee_in_paise` and `services_total_in_paise` are plain columns, trusted from whatever gets inserted, with no trigger deriving them independently. `grand_total_in_paise` can't be tampered with (it's a generated column, summing the two), but the two things it sums are themselves currently unprotected. This is the concrete gap Om's security question surfaces — worth closing with a trigger that derives these from the rate tables directly, once those tables (Section 6) exist.

**The escape hatch, needed because businesses have waivers.** A rule that always recomputes and overwrites, with no exceptions, can't accommodate a legitimate manual override — so the design needs a narrow, auditable way to deviate from the computed number, not an all-or-nothing choice between "always trust the computed value" and "trust whatever the client sends." None of what follows exists in the schema yet — this whole section is a proposal, not a description of current behavior.

**Concretely, table by table.** On `visits`: leave `consultation_fee_in_paise`, `services_total_in_paise`, and `grand_total_in_paise` exactly as they are — `grand_total_in_paise` keeps its current job, always the honest sum of the two, generated, untouchable. Add four new columns: `final_amount_in_paise` (nullable integer — null means nothing overridden, the real charge is `grand_total_in_paise` as normal), `override_reason` (text, nullable), `override_by` (uuid, references `profiles(id)`, nullable), `override_status` (text, would need a CHECK in `'none'/'pending'/'approved'`, default `'none'`). Whatever actually gets billed reads as `COALESCE(final_amount_in_paise, grand_total_in_paise)`. On `packages`: needs more than override columns, because there's nowhere to represent a cancellation at all today — `status` only allows `'Active'/'Completed'/'Expired'`, no `'Cancelled'` yet, so that needs adding first. Then the same shape: `computed_refund_in_paise` (backend-derived from the confirmed formula), `final_refund_in_paise` (nullable override), `override_reason`, `override_by`, `override_status` — same pattern, same COALESCE logic for what actually gets refunded.

A trigger would enforce that `final_amount_in_paise` (or `final_refund_in_paise`) can only differ from the computed value when `override_by` references a profile with `role = 'admin'` — the same check `validate_owner_is_admin()` already performs elsewhere, reused rather than reinvented. This is a design recommendation, not an existing constraint.

**Why nullable, not defaulted to the computed value — compared directly.** Considered: instead of `final_amount_in_paise` starting NULL, have it default to `grand_total_in_paise` at insert time, so every row always carries a concrete number. Rejected, for a concrete staleness reason: if a visit's `grand_total_in_paise` ever legitimately changes later (a genuine data-entry correction, unrelated to any waiver), a defaulted `final_amount_in_paise` would stay frozen at its original value and silently disagree with the now-current `grand_total_in_paise` — indistinguishable from a real override when queried later. Staying NULL avoids this entirely: `COALESCE(final_amount_in_paise, grand_total_in_paise)` always reflects the live `grand_total_in_paise` for any row that was never actually overridden, automatically, with no extra trigger work needed to keep them in sync. If the query-ergonomics of always typing `COALESCE` gets annoying later, a small view (or a second generated column) exposing that same expression as one plain column is a cheap addition at that point — worth doing once this is actually being built, not a reason to default the column today.

**Data-shape constraint, and a separate authorization layer.** A CHECK constraint, same style as `chk_referral_doctor_info`/`chk_consultation_type` elsewhere in this schema — several columns required to move together, three-way given the pending/approved states: `status='none'` requires `final_amount_in_paise`/`override_reason`/`override_by` all NULL; `status='pending'` requires `final_amount_in_paise` still NULL (nothing charged differently yet) but `override_reason` filled in and `override_by` still NULL; `status='approved'` requires all three filled in. This only proves the DATA is internally consistent, not that the right PERSON wrote it — a separate trigger is needed to check the acting user (`auth.uid()`, never trusted from client input) actually has `role = 'admin'` before allowing a move to `'approved'`, and to derive `override_by` from `auth.uid()` itself rather than accepting whatever id a client submits for that column — otherwise the CHECK constraint only proves some admin's id is present, not that the person performing this specific approval is that admin.

**Refund preview — who computes the number shown before confirming.** Should come from the same calculation the backend uses when the cancellation is actually finalized, not a second copy of the same math living only in the frontend. Concretely: clicking "Cancel Package" would call a small backend function (days attended → refund, the confirmed regular-rate formula) purely to preview, writing nothing yet; confirming calls that exact same function for real. Same code path both times, so preview and reality can't quietly drift apart from each other later if the formula ever changes.

**On workflow speed.** None of this needs to touch the ordinary fast path. A receptionist doing a normal visit — no waiver, nothing unusual — never sees an override field or a reason box; the form is exactly as fast as today, because `override_status` just sits at its default. The extra fields only appear when someone specifically requests a waiver, as a small secondary action next to the computed total, not a permanent addition to the main form. On requests piling up if the admin isn't around: a pending request never blocks anything — while `override_status = 'pending'`, the visit is still billed at the full computed amount, a complete and valid transaction on its own. The waiver, whenever it's approved, retroactively adjusts an already-closed transaction — an ordinary thing for a business to do, not a broken state.

**Three scopes of override, same mechanism, different lifetimes:**
- *Transactional* (one visit or refund gets a one-off adjustment): the `final_amount_in_paise`/`override_*` columns above, on that one row. Needed regardless of anything else here — this is the baseline case.
- *Standing, per-patient* — **redesigned; a single flat rate isn't enough.** A single `override_amount_in_paise` can only represent "the total is always exactly X, no matter what" — it can't represent a PARTIAL override on one fee component (consultation waived but normal daily rate; normal consultation but a discounted daily rate), and Om confirmed real cases like that exist. Redesigned as one row per component being overridden, not one row per patient: `patient_id`, `override_type` (text + CHECK, same fake-enum convention as everywhere else in this schema — `'CONSULTATION_FEE'` / `'THERAPY_RATE'` / `'FLAT_TOTAL'`), `override_amount_in_paise`, `reason`, `granted_by`, `granted_at`, `expires_at` (nullable — blank for indefinite, a date for something meant to lapse), `is_active`. A patient can have up to two component rows active at once (consultation + therapy independently), or one `FLAT_TOTAL` row — kept mutually exclusive by convention for now rather than a cross-row DB constraint, since that's awkward to enforce with a plain CHECK and this edge case hasn't come up yet. Rows over columns-on-`patients`, specifically because each grant deserves its own reason and history — a consultation waiver from two years ago and a three-month therapy discount granted later are genuinely different decisions that shouldn't have to share (or triplicate) reason/granted_by/expires_at fields. Whatever computes a visit's baseline price checks this table first, per component, before falling back to normal tier rates — set once, automatic after that. **Open, not assumed:** Om's own ₹180 case wasn't specified precisely enough to pick — is it `FLAT_TOTAL` (ignore the normal calculation entirely, always charge 180, regardless of complaint count or gap-return penalties), or `CONSULTATION_FEE=0` + `THERAPY_RATE=180` (which would still scale normally with more than one complaint)? These behave differently the moment a second complaint is involved — worth confirming which matches what actually happens.
- *Promotional* (a blanket discount for a day or an event — the Mother's Day example): not worth dedicated structure yet. Treat it as the ordinary transactional override, applied by hand to each qualifying visit as it happens. A real "promotions" concept (date ranges, eligibility rules, overlap handling) is genuine complexity for something that's happened once so far — build it if and when it becomes a recurring pattern.

**On "is a random number secure":** no, a bare number with nothing checking it isn't secure, whether it's 0, 350, or 600 — but the same number becomes secure the moment the schema requires any deviation from the computed total to carry a valid `override_by` from an admin. The number was never the risk; an unverified claim that a deviation is authorized was.

**How `override_by` gets proven, not just claimed — three options:**
- *A shared code word typed into a field:* weakest, ruled out. Structurally identical to trusting the price itself — anyone who learns the word can trigger it directly from outside the UI, and it can't distinguish which admin approved anything.
- *Live TOTP (Google Authenticator) verification of a specific admin, without switching sessions:* real and buildable — Supabase exposes an admin-level API to verify a named user's authenticator code from the backend, independent of that user's own active session, so a small server-side function could check "admin X, code 123456" directly against admin X's enrolled factor without the receptionist's browser session ever being touched. The cost: this needs actual backend code deployed outside the database (a small serverless/edge function holding a credential that must never reach the browser) — the first piece of this project that wouldn't live entirely in SQL and triggers. A real step up in scope from anything else discussed so far.
- *Request-and-approve, no live identity verification at all:* receptionist flags `override requested` with a reason at the computed price; the admin, from their own separate session, later updates that same row — permitted purely because their profile's `role = 'admin'`, the same check used throughout this schema already. Recommended for now, since it costs nothing beyond columns + an RLS rule, versus TOTP's genuinely new category of backend work. Worth reconsidering later if instant, in-the-moment approval turns out to matter enough to justify building it.

## 8. Packages — Attendance Tracking, Verified Against the Real Schema

**Confirmed by reading the actual migration file, not memory, after a citation mistake earlier in this session made that discipline worth re-establishing.**

`packages` carries both `clinic_id` (direct FK to `clinics`, validated against `patient_clinic_access` — the standard tenancy check every location-specific table gets) and `linked_complaint_id` (FK to `complaint_courses`). Not one or the other — both, doing different jobs: `clinic_id` for location-scoping, `linked_complaint_id` for the actual clinical relationship.

**The real finding:** `duration_days`, `attended_days`, `missed_days`, and `expiry_date` are all plain stored values — not generated, not trigger-derived. `packages` has exactly two triggers: one stamps `updated_at`, the other (`packages_clinic_id_guard`) validates `clinic_id`/`patient_id` against `patient_clinic_access` and has nothing to do with attendance. `visits` has no `package_id` column at all — no FK, no trigger, nothing anywhere connects a specific visit to a specific package. `attended_days`/`missed_days`/`day_log` are entirely app-maintained counters with zero structural connection to real visit records; nothing in the database would catch two visits logged against one package while `attended_days` only got bumped once, or a deleted visit whose count never decremented.

**Refinement, in priority order:**
1. Add `visits.package_id uuid references packages(id)` (nullable — plenty of visits aren't against a package at all). Once it exists, `attended_days` can become something a trigger keeps correct — incremented whenever a visit with that `package_id` is inserted, the same discipline `clinics.invoice_counter` already gets. This closes the main hole: today nothing stops `attended_days` from silently drifting from what actually happened.
2. `missed_days` is genuinely harder — a missed day has no visit row to count, so it can't be derived the same way. Better computed at read time (elapsed eligible days since `purchase_date`, adjusted for `exclude_sundays`, minus `attended_days`) than stored and incremented — but this depends on exactly how "missed" gets decided in the real workflow (only once a day is unambiguously past with nothing logged, or can staff mark a day missed in advance?), which isn't clear enough yet to commit to one design.
3. `day_log` has the same underlying issue as `attended_days`/`missed_days` — untouched by any trigger, a third independent representation of the same fact. Worth eventually picking one source of truth (`day_log` as the real record, the two counters becoming cached aggregates derived from it) rather than three peers that can silently disagree.
4. Lower priority: no CHECK constraint stops `attended_days` from exceeding `duration_days` or going negative — worth adding once the derivation mechanism above is settled, as a backstop, not a fix on its own.

**Where the "pending request" state lives, concretely:** no separate table — the `override_status` column specified above, on `visits` or `packages` directly, whichever row needs the price. A receptionist-initiated request is one INSERT: `final_amount_in_paise` left equal to the computed total (nothing charged differently yet), `override_status = 'pending'`, `override_reason` filled in, `override_by` empty. The admin's approval is a plain UPDATE to that same row: `final_amount_in_paise` changes, `override_status → 'approved'`, `override_by` filled with their own id, permitted only because of their role. An admin acting live, on the spot: identical columns, written in one INSERT instead of two, `override_status` going straight to `'approved'`. One mechanism covers both "ask and wait" and "admin does it themselves in the moment."
